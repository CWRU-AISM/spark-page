Abstract

Changes in a robot's observations do not automatically imply changes in the task it is solving, but language-conditioned manipulation systems often fail to separate the two. If an object moves, for example, the locations at which actions should be executed may change while the underlying manipulation plan remains valid. We introduce Sequential Planning via Anchored Robotic Keypoints (Spark), which separates a task's symbolic plan from its execution-time perceptual grounding, and we experimentally isolate whether a fixed symbolic plan can survive substantial changes to the scene through execution-time re-grounding. SPARK represents the task as a symbolic behavior tree whose object references are grounded to current 3D observations during execution, allowing the robot to update where actions are performed while preserving the plan. Runtime verification then enables local recovery and renewed grounding, only resorting to a new plan if necessary. To test the separation between plan and perceptual grounding, we perform a set of manipulation experiments with a fixed plan while moving objects during execution. Under a 10 cm displacement, verification and adaptation maintain 66.7% task success, compared with 2.2% when disabled, demonstrating the importance of the underlying principle. Without task-specific model training and with an open-weight planner, SPARK also achieves 66.8% mean success across six LIBERO-PRO settings and 68% across eleven physical task-embodiment settings on three robot platforms. Together, these results show that robustness does not require re-planning the task and for an important class of failures, it is sufficient to preserve the plan and update the system's perception of a changing world.

Composing the score

SAM3 grounds the scene from the platform cameras. One language-model call writes the whole plan as a typed behavior tree, the score, and the robot follows it without task-specific model training. The LIBERO-PRO results use the open-weight Qwen3.8-27B, served locally; the physical robots use Gemini. Each action refers to an object by label, which is resolved to a current 3D position when the action executes. A changed instruction may invalidate the task plan, while a moved object may instead invalidate only the perceptual grounding of an otherwise valid plan. “Put the bowl on the plate” calls for the same score whether the bowl starts on the left or the right. Only the pixels that the label bowl binds to have moved. SPARK revises the level of representation that the change actually invalidates: when the task structure remains valid, renewed perception and local execution are sufficient without solving the task again.

Each skill checks a post-condition. A failed check triggers a three-tier recovery procedure: an in-place correction, such as a small motion and grasp adjustment; perception re-grounding, which retracts the arm by 10 cm, re-runs SAM3, and retries the same plan using updated object locations; and plan regeneration through a new language-model call only after the execution attempt is exhausted. The first two tiers preserve the existing behavior tree and require no additional LLM call. Five base primitives (move_to_keypoint, move_relative, grasp, release, wait) cover basic motion operations, and more than thirty registered skills add calibrated closed-loop routines such as force-feedback descent, contact detection, and local retry logic. In simulation, an additional language-model call proposes three alternative prompts per object and SAM3 confidence selects the detection; in the matched ablation this raises spatial success by 27.7 points and object success by 10.0, but lowers goal-task success by 8.4. Physical experiments use single-prompt detection.

The object slides over 40 simulator steps by 0 to 10 cm on the same seeded schedule for both conditions; the planner is not in the loop.

At 10 cm, success is 30/45 (66.7%) with verification and adaptation, compared with 1/45 (2.2%) when both are disabled. The difference grows from six successful trials at 0 cm to twenty-nine at 10 cm. Because the plans are fixed, execution checks and recovery preserve success while retaining the same plan.

CaP-Bench (Robosuite)

Per-task success (%). SPARK and CaP-Agent0 use 100 trials per task; RATS uses its 50-trial skill-transfer evaluation. SPARK averages 63.4% against 68.3% for CaP-Agent0's three-model ensemble, succeeding in 100% of Lift and CubeRestack trials and 97% of Stack trials, while success falls to 60% for Wipe, 63% for TwoArmLift, and 0% for NutAssemblySquare.
